# Chapter 1 · The Wooden Machine

*Why read the Abhidhamma through a machine at all?*

---

Picture a puppet carved from wood. Strings run from its joints to a frame above. Pull them, and the puppet walks. Pull
again, and it stands still. From the back of the hall it looks for a moment as though it wants to walk, as though it
has somewhere to go.

About fifteen hundred years ago, a monk named Buddhaghosa used this picture to explain the oldest and hardest part of
the Buddha's teaching. His book, the *Visuddhimagga*, or *Path of Purification*, says that a wooden machine
(*dāruyanta*) is *"empty, lifeless, without desire"*. Yet by the joining of wood and string it walks and stands, and it
*"seems to have desire and activity"*. Then he turns the picture around: so too mentality and materiality, mind and
matter, by joining with each other, walk and stand, and seem to have desire and activity.[^1]

The verse he quotes next is short:

> *Mind and matter are really here,*
> *but no being, no human, is found in them.*
> *Empty, this is a fabricated machine.*[^2]

The standard English translation calls the wooden machine a *marionette*, a figure worked by strings. That is the right
picture. A marionette is the simplest machine there is: parts, a joining, and movement that looks like intention.

This book borrows that picture and changes one thing about it. The wood becomes silicon. The string becomes code.

## The question the puppet answers

The *Abhidhamma* is the part of the Buddha's teaching that says what experience is made of, moment by moment. It is
built from lists: kinds of mind, kinds of mental factor, kinds of matter, and the ways each conditions the others. It
is precise, and it is hard to read.

The puppet answers one question the lists raise. If there is no self inside a person, only mind and matter, then who
walks? Who reaches for the cup, says the word, turns at the sound of a name?

A later commentary on the *Visuddhimagga* states the objection plainly: *if there is no self, how does mere mind and
matter accomplish going and the other things a self would do?*[^3] The puppet is the answer. Walking does not need a
walker inside. It needs parts, and a joining.

So the machine is not there to make mind sound mechanical. It is there to show how action happens without a self, and
the commentators say what it is for: to clear the view, and to wear away the perception of a being.[^4]

## Who pulls the strings?

A puppet has a puppeteer. Someone stands above the stage. If the picture needs one, has the self come back in?

The commentaries saw this coming, and they named the puppeteer. It is mind.

The commentary on the Buddha's long discourse about mindfulness says that the body is a machine worked *"by the
string of mind"*, and that mind is its charioteer.[^5] A sub-commentary says the machine of the body runs *"by the
power of mind, though no self is its agent"*.[^6] And when someone objects that a boat or an arrow moves by a person's
force, the answer is: let it be so. The person is compounded too, made of parts and conditions, so *"all the more is it
mere phenomena, not a doctrine of a person"*.[^7]

So there is a puppeteer. It is mind, and mind is not a self. It is a stream of moments, each one conditioned, each one
gone as the next arrives. The strings are pulled, but not by anyone standing outside the conditions.

This matters for everything that follows. The simile never says *no one does anything*. That view has a name in the
early texts. A teacher called Makkhali taught that there is *"no self-doing, no human effort"*, and that beings simply
run their course like a ball of string unwinding until it is done.[^8] The Buddha rejected it. When asked whether there
is such a thing as self-doing, he answered that *"there is an element of initiative"*, and that this is what beings'
own doing means.[^9]

The way through is a distinction the tradition makes again and again: two ways of speaking, both true. In the
ultimate sense, *"there is no doer of kamma"*, no self behind the act. In the conventional sense, the sense the world
agrees on, there is a doer, and we say so.[^10] The puppet walks. You walked here. Both sentences are true at their own
level. This book will lean on that distinction often, and it starts here.

## Machines, again and again

The wooden machine is not the only one. The tradition reaches for machines again and again, each time to carry one
lesson.

In the *Therīgāthā*, the verses of the early nuns, a nun named Subhā is on her way to a mango grove when a man blocks her
path.[^subha] She answers him with a puppet. Take a doll on strings and pegs, she says, and pull it apart into its strings and
pegs. Where, then, would you set your mind?[^11]

In the *Theragāthā*, the verses of the early monks, the body turns *"worked by the machine of kamma"*, and the Dhamma is
*"the smasher of the kamma-machine"*.[^12]

In the *Visuddhimagga*, the meditation on the four elements, earth, water, fire and air, ends with the body seen as
*"this machine of elements"*, running on and *"deceiving the foolish with the appearance of man and woman, like a magic
show"*.[^13]

And the wooden machine itself is one simile among about a dozen for the same lesson. A sub-commentary lists them: the
chariot, the house, the fist, the lute, the army, the tree, the wooden machine, two sheaves of reeds leaning on each
other, the drum, the blind man carrying the cripple, the boat and its crew.[^14] Buddhaghosa says it himself, right
after the puppet: this should be shown *"not only by the wooden-machine simile, but by other similes too"*.[^15]

So the commentators did not choose the puppet because it was the most advanced machine they knew. They knew others,
and used them for other lessons: a water wheel for beings turning through birth after birth, a simile of five
sugar-cane mills in the Abhidhamma's own commentary, a wooden elephant with sixty men hidden inside it.[^machines] They
chose the puppet because it carried one lesson well.

The Indian world around them used the same picture for the opposite lesson. In the *Bhagavad Gītā*, the Lord dwells in
the heart of all beings and turns them *"as if mounted on a machine, by illusion"*.[^gita] There, the machine has an
operator, and the operator is the point. The Buddhist use deliberately takes the operator out: the machine runs, and
when you look for the one who runs it, you find more machine.

## A new machine

This book adds one more machine to that long habit: the computer.

It is not chosen because it is the newest. It is chosen because it carries three things the old machines could not
say as clearly.

```
WHAT THE COMPUTER CARRIES        WHERE THIS BOOK USES IT

a fixed instruction set          every moment of mind uses the same
                                 small set of slots         (Chapter 5)

compiling                        perception builds a whole world out
                                 of moments                 (Chapters 2–4)

decompiling                      insight takes that world apart into
                                 its moments again          (Chapter 16)
```

Everything else the computer seems to offer, the old machines already said. A puppet already shows action without a
self. A chariot wheel touching the ground at one point already shows life lasting a single moment.[^wheel] Subhā's doll, pulled apart,
already shows a whole taken back to its parts. Where the computer only repeats them, this book will say so, and give
the older picture its due.

So the change from the marionette is small and exact. The wood, the matter, becomes silicon. The string, the mind that
moves it, becomes code. And the book keeps one working motto from the start: **we compile the account, not the world.**
A simile is a way of understanding what the texts say. It is never a way of deciding what is real.

## The objection, carried

There is a serious objection to all of this, and it does not go away.

Every age explains the mind with its newest machine. In the age of clockwork, the mind was clockwork. In the age of
steam and pipes, it had pressures that built up and needed release. Now it is a computer. The philosopher John Searle
put it simply: *"because we do not understand the brain very well we are constantly tempted to use the latest
technology as a model for trying to understand it."*[^16]

The wooden machine does not answer this objection. It only shows that Buddhaghosa did the same thing with the
machines of his day. A precedent is not a defense.

What the precedent does give is a test. **A claim in this book is covered by the old simile only if its lesson
survives when you swap the silicon back for wood.** *No self is needed for action* survives the swap: the puppet already
says it. *Every moment of mind uses the same fixed slots* does not survive the swap; the puppet never said it. Claims
of that second kind have to earn their place on their own, chapter by chapter, against the texts. Each chapter ends by
showing where the computer fails, because those are the places where the texts say something the machine cannot.

## Others who have looked

This is not the first book to read the Buddha's teaching through a computer. In 1991, the Thai teacher Buddhadāsa
Bhikkhu gave a talk called *"Living Computer is Not-Self"*. Scholars have traced the mechanical figures of Buddhist
literature, the robots and puppets and moving dolls, and how they teach agency without a self.[^17] Others have built
formal and computational models of the Abhidhamma's moments of mind.[^18] An earlier study in this same library,
*The Wheel That Unwinds the Wheel*, reads the Abhidhamma as a process that can be written down precisely enough to
run.[^19]

What this book adds is the simile itself, taken all the way through, with its breaks kept on the page.

## Where the simile breaks

**Software is a pattern of its hardware. Mind is not a pattern of matter.** Right after the wooden machine, Buddhaghosa
adds a second picture: a drum and its sound. The drum is one thing, the sound another; they are not mixed. *"Mind is
empty of matter, and matter is empty of mind."*[^20] Here the old picture fits better than the new one. Wood and string
are two materials joined. Software is a state of the machine it runs on. Whenever this book says *software*, read it as
*the string*, never as something the hardware secretes.

**A program runs on its own once it starts. A puppet moves only while it is pulled.** A stored program that runs to the
end is closer to Makkhali's ball of string unwinding than to Buddhaghosa's puppet. The Dhamma keeps initiative real:
each moment of intention is a fresh pull on the string, not a line in a script written long ago.

**A computer has a designer and a purpose. This machine has neither.** No one built it, and it is not for anything.
That is the deepest break, and the last chapter returns to it.

**A puppet's emptiness is obvious. A computer's is argued over.** When Buddhaghosa pointed at the marionette, everyone
in the hall knew there was no one inside it. Today, whether anyone is inside a computer is a live question. The old
lesson is easier to see in wood, and this book will keep returning to the wood to see it.

So the book keeps the old lesson and borrows the new machine. Wherever the new machine says more than the old, it has
to earn it.
