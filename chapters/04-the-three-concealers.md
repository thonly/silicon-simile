# Chapter 4 · The Three Concealers

*Why does the world look solid, lasting and safe?*

---

In a dark yard, someone whirls a burning stick. You see a ring of fire. There is no ring. There is one small flame, in one place at a time, and your eyes tell you it is everywhere on a circle at once.

Chapter 3 met this picture and used it for one thing: how a stream of separate moments can seem to be one lasting mind. This chapter asks the larger question the picture was made for. The Abhidhamma says everything we meet is made of moments that arise and pass away, that nothing in it is a lasting thing, and that none of it is the self it seems to serve. Yet the world does not look that way. It looks solid, it looks lasting, and it looks like a safe place to stand.

Why?

## The question, asked of a meditator

The *Visuddhimagga* asks it in a place where you might not expect it.[^1] Its author has been leading a meditator, step by step, to the **knowledge of rise and fall**: the moment-by-moment seeing that each thing arises and then passes away. The meditator has cleared away the ten corruptions that spoil that knowledge. And still, the book says, the three marks of existence do not show. Impermanence, unsatisfactoriness and not-self stay hidden, even here.

Why do they not appear? The text gives one answer three times over: *"by not attending, and because something conceals each one."*[^1]

- Impermanence is hidden by **continuity** (*santati*).
- Suffering is hidden by **the postures** (*iriyāpatha*).
- Not-self is hidden by **compactness** (*ghana*).

Each mark appears when its concealer is broken. This chapter takes the three in turn. The book calls them **concealers** because that is what the text calls them, and because the word keeps one thing straight: they do not hide anything from a machine. They hide the marks from a being, and only a stream of mind has them.

First, one small word needs care. *Amanasikārā*, "by not attending", is not a switch someone flips. The sub-commentary reads it as a cause: the marks stay hidden *because* of the not-attending. It adds that the concealers themselves come to be through it.[^2] Take away the not-attending, and the concealers are not a second thing waiting to be removed. That matters later.

## The first concealer: continuity

Every moment of mind arises and passes away. You do not see it, because the next moment arises at once, in the very place where the last one ended. Chapter 3 gave the rule: each mind breaks up only as the condition for the next. So what shows is the presence of the new one, and the absence of the old one is covered.

The sub-commentary says this plainly, and then says why the picture of the wheel fits: because the later mind's arising covers the earlier one's end, the "being" side shows and the "not being" side does not, and the ordinary person takes it all for one thing.[^3] *"This is made plain by the sight of the firebrand wheel."*

The mark that is hidden is **impermanence**. What breaks the concealer is the thing the meditator was already doing: *gathering up the rise and fall*.[^1]

Here the computer offers a rough likeness, and only a rough one. A name in a program stays the same while the contents under it are overwritten at every step. The name `x` seems to be one thing. It is a different value each time. The likeness is the seeming of *one lasting thing* over a run of replacements. It fails in places the end of the chapter lists, and the old picture, the whirled stick, does the teaching better.

### What the wheel does in the texts

The wheel appears in the texts for three different jobs, and a careful reader should know which is meant.

1. **Presence covers absence.** This is the job above. A later moment's arising makes the earlier one's ending unseen.[^3]
2. **The mind door joins earlier sights.** Ledi Sayadaw, a modern commentator on the manual, uses the wheel for another purpose. A man swirls a torch in the dark, another man sees it and takes it for a wheel. In the same way, he says, the mind door gathers earlier sights, which are visible forms, into one shape and one continuity.[^4] This is about matter seen, not about the stream of mind itself.
3. **After insight.** Once rise and fall have been seen, the *Visuddhimagga* lists the whirled brand among the pictures of how formations then look: empty, without core.[^5] Here the wheel is no longer a concealer. It is what the unconcealed looks like.

### What the eye does

A reader will ask whether the ring is just "persistence of vision". It is better not to say so. That phrase was used to explain how film seems to move, and film scholars have called it their myth of creation.[^6] The present account of a spun light is different: the brain keeps a streak along the path of a moving light, and the streak exists in the brain's own picture of the stimulus and not in the light.[^7] That takes tens of milliseconds. By the texts' own pace, many hundreds of thousands of koṭis of minds arise in a single finger-snap (chapter 3), so tens of milliseconds holds a countless number of moments. The ring is a pleasant likeness for how a succession can look like a whole, and it is no evidence about moments.

The texts give no mechanism of the eye at all. They say what the covering is, and they stop there.

## The second concealer: the postures

The second answer is odd at first. Suffering is hidden by the postures.

Sit still long enough and the body hurts. You shift. The hurt goes. A sub-commentary states the order exactly: a new posture is the remover of the pain that arose in the posture before it, and so seems to hide it.[^8] The relief is real. It is also what keeps one thing from being read.

What is hidden is not this ache or that. It is the *constant oppression* that the passing of every formation brings with it, the way the whole thing is being worn down moment after moment (*abhiṇhasampaṭipīḷana*). Each shift answers a local pain and so lets the universal one go unread.[^1] The mark that is hidden is **suffering**, in the sense of the texts: the unsatisfactoriness of what is always passing.

What breaks the concealer is *attending to that constant oppression*. The posture is not removed. It is seen through.

The computer's rough likeness is fault masking. A system retries a failed step, or fails over to a second machine, each fault answered quietly, so the log never reaches the user. Everything works. The faults happened.

## The third concealer: compactness

The third is the lump. Parts that were never one thing are taken as one, and what hides is **not-self**.

The text's word is *ghana*, which means a dense mass, a solid. A thing seems compact when its parts are not examined, and the seeming comes from grasping: the oneness is what a grasping mind makes of parts it has not looked at.[^8] When the parts are *separated* (*vinibbhoga*), by knowledge, the lump does not hold: the sub-commentary says it melts like a lump of foam rubbed by the hand. The earth element is one thing, the water element another; contact is one thing, feeling another.[^8] No self is left to be found among them.

The sub-commentaries sort compactness into modes. The Abhidhamma sub-commentaries name four: **continuity, grouping, function and object** (*santati, samūha, kicca, ārammaṇa*).[^9] Notice that the first is the first concealer. In that scheme continuity is itself a kind of compactness, and some sub-commentaries count it among the lumps that hide not-self.[^10] The *Visuddhimagga*'s own list has three concealers, with continuity apart, and the sub-commentary on that passage counts three modes of compactness and not four.[^8]

The count is not the same in every text. The book gives the *Visuddhimagga*'s three and names the others.

The computer's likeness is a program linked into one file with the boundaries between its parts stripped. You get one thing with one name. The undo is not to rebuild the source. The text's word is *separate*: list the parts, and say which is which. That is nearer to taking a program apart than to decompiling it.

## They are not equally deep

The three do not stand level. A commentary on the Abhidhamma says that the marks of impermanence and suffering appear whether or not a Buddha has arisen, and that the mark of not-self does not appear without one.[^11] Even ascetics of great power can say "impermanent, suffering". They cannot say "not-self".

So the third concealer is the hardest. The reason the text gives is not that it is cleverly built. It is that no one has seen through it without being taught.

## Nobody set these

It is tempting to say the concealers were set up on purpose, like a build setting with a switch labeled *attention*. The texts do not say so, for three reasons.

First, there is no chooser. Chapter 18 returns to this: the machine in this book is for nothing, and it has no maker's intention behind its run.

Second, attention is not absent. Attention is one of the seven mental states that arise with every mind (chapter 5). What differs is *where it is pointed*. Where attention is not pointed at rise and fall, at the constant oppression, or at the parts, the three concealments hold. Where it is pointed there, with the conditions that make that possible, the concealment breaks.

Third, **broken** is not **deleted**. The text says a mark appears when its concealer is broken, lifted or separated. It does not say the concealer is removed from the world. Insight is a long training and the third mark is the hardest. The computer's likeness here is a rough one too: programs that have been optimized tend to be harder to read back. That is a likeness, and it is not the reason.

## Where the simile breaks

The chapter has used four rough likenesses, and every one breaks.

**A name that stays while its contents change** is a feature someone wrote. The continuity of mind was written by no one. The likeness also hands the lesson back to the old picture, the whirled stick, which is why the book keeps the stick first.

**Fault masking** is designed. A failover is built by someone who meant it to hide faults. A posture is not. The relief is real, and the error is only in reading it as the end of the oppression underneath. Nobody played a trick on the sufferer.

**A linked program** has a whole that is really there. The linker makes it. In the texts, the compact whole is taken for one by grasping parts that were never examined, and the parts are the only things there. And the name that the whole carries is, in this book's own telling, perception's mark (chapter 2), not the linker's.

**The machine is not fooled by its own scheduler.** A computer hides nothing from itself. The concealers hide the marks of existence from a stream, and only a stream has them. Whatever a program does, it is not *failing to attend*.

The swap test holds. Put the whirled stick, the shifting body and a pile of unexamined parts where the computer was, and every lesson in this chapter survives. The computer was never needed here. It earns its place in the chapters that follow, where mind turns out to have something like an instruction set.
