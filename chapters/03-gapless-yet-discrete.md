# Chapter 3 · Gapless, Yet Discrete

*Can a stream have no gaps and still be made of moments?*

---

Close a laptop's lid at night and open it in the morning. The page you were writing is still there, the cursor
blinking after the last word you typed. For the machine, nothing has happened. Its next instruction follows its last
one, with none between them. The clock on the wall says eight hours have gone by.

Keep that picture. It is the one thing in this chapter the computer adds, and it comes near the end.

The Abhidhamma says two things about the mind that sound as if they cannot both be true. The mind is made of moments,
each one arising and passing away on its own. And the stream of those moments has no gaps. A stream of separate pieces
with nothing between them can sound like a riddle, or like a contradiction politely phrased.

It is neither. Seeing why takes the texts' own rule, some care with two words, and an old lamp.

## Two words

**Discrete** means made of separate members, each with its own place: this moment, then that one.

**Gapless** needs more care, because it has two meanings. A mathematician hears a line with no holes in it, a
*continuum*, where between any two points there are always more. This book means something plainer: **adjacent**.
Nothing comes between one moment and the next.

Read that way, the riddle is already half gone.

## The rule

The Paṭṭhāna, the last of the Abhidhamma's seven books, sets out twenty-four ways in which one thing conditions
another. One of them is the **proximity condition** (*anantarapaccaya*). Its rule:
*"whatever states of mind arise immediately after whatever states, those states are their condition by proximity."*[^1]

Each moment of mind, as it ceases, is the condition for the next to arise. It hands nothing over. A sub-commentary
puts it exactly: a mind breaks up, moment by moment, *only* as the proximity condition of the next.[^2] Its ending is
the next one's arising.

The words *nothing between them* are the commentary's. Buddhaghosa, in the *Visuddhimagga*, explains the condition's
name: *"there is nothing between them, so they are proximate."* And they are *thoroughly* proximate, he adds, because
mind has no shape.[^3] Things with shape, set side by side, meet at edges, and one can say which lies below, which above
and which across. The Abhidhamma's root sub-commentary, the *Mūlaṭīkā*, says moments of mind have no such division:
they have no shape, and the one that conditions and the one it conditions are never present together.[^4] There is no
edge for a seam to run along.

## A next needs an order

Here is the whole of the answer.

*Next* is a word that only works in an order of separate members. Take a line drawn with a pen and pick a point on it.
Which point comes next? None does. Between any two points on a line lie others, as many as you like. Aristotle made
the argument in his *Physics*: a continuous line is not built out of points, and no point is next to another.[^5]

So the texts' claim needs discreteness. A stream in which every moment has a next one, with nothing between, has to be
made of separate moments. *Gapless* and *discrete* are not in tension. Gapless, in this sense, requires discrete.

The texts give the stream's pace in their own terms: *"in a single finger-snap, many hundreds of thousands of koṭis of
minds arise."*[^6] A koṭi is ten million. The figure is theirs, and this book leaves it as they gave it.

## The river between

If the mind is a row of moments, what runs between one thought and the next, or in dreamless sleep? The texts answer
that the row does not stop. When no process of seeing, hearing or thinking is running, a kind of mind still flows: the
**life-continuum** (*bhavaṅga*), the same kind of mind that began this life, taking the same object, moment after
moment. The manual says it flows *"unbroken, like a river's stream."*[^7] Processes of perception run *"interspersed with the life-continuum"*, so the stream is unbroken
from the first moment of a life to the last.[^8]

Chapter 6 shows how the processes and the river take turns.

## Across every gap

The texts carry the rule beyond a single life, and this is where it surprises.

**Across death.** At the end of a life the last mind, the death mind, arises and ceases. *"Immediately after it"*, the
mind of the new existence arises.[^9] A later sub-commentary presses the point against those who held that a being
passes through an in-between state on the way. Death and rebirth may lie thousands of leagues apart, and still the one
is the proximity condition of the other: *"no distance in place."* A death mind may condition a rebirth mind thousands
of eons later, and still there is *"no distance in time."*[^10]

**Across the mindless existence.** Some beings, the texts say, are reborn with no mind at all, as matter only, for
many hundreds of eons. These are the *non-percipient* beings. When that life ends and mind arises again, what conditions
it? A sub-commentary answers: *"even a mind long ceased is a proximity condition, because nothing of its kind arises in
between."*[^11]

**Across cessation.** The deepest of the meditative attainments, *cessation*, stops mind and its factors for a time.
On emerging, the Paṭṭhāna says, the last mind before cessation is the proximity condition of the first mind after
it.[^12]

**One exception.** The last mind of an *arahant*, one who has ended rebirth, has no next. A commentary gives the power
to condition the next mind to *"any mind that has just ceased, excepting an arahant's death mind."*[^13] There the
stream ends. Chapter 18 returns to that end.

## Order, and time

So the order holds even where, by any clock, days or eons go by. That raises a question the commentators did not settle
together: is the stretch between those moments *time*?

**Buddhaghosa answers that it is.** Some teachers had said the proximity condition means nearness in time. He refutes
them with cessation: there, *"by the power of development, there is no immediacy in time; we say this too."*[^14] Days
pass between the last mind and the next, so proximity cannot be a matter of time. It is a matter of order.

**The Mūlaṭīkā answers that it is not.** Time, it says, is only a way of speaking about states as they occur. Across
cessation, and across the mindless existence, no state of mind occurs, and the matter that goes on in the body is
another continuum, which makes no interval for mind. So *"there is no interval of time"* at all.[^15]

In Pāli the two answers differ by a syllable: *kālānantaratā natthi*, "there is no immediacy in time", and
*kālantaratā natthi*, "there is no interval of time". They say opposite things.

This book names both readings, and decides nothing between them.

## The older pictures, first

Chapter 1 promised that wherever an older picture already teaches a lesson, the book would say so. Here three do.

**The lamp.** In the *Milindapañha*, a dialogue between the monk Nāgasena and King Milinda, the king asks whether the
one who arises is the same as before, or another. Nāgasena asks him about a lamp kept burning all night. Is the flame
in the first watch the flame in the middle watch? No, says the king. Then was there one lamp in the first watch and
another in the middle and another in the last? No, the king says: it burned all night depending on the same lamp.
*"Just so,"* says Nāgasena, *"the stream of states joins. One arises, another ceases; it joins as if without before or
after."* So it is *"neither the same, nor another."*[^16]

**The firebrand wheel.** In the first sutta of the *Dīgha Nikāya*, a thinker sees the eye and the other senses break up and
concludes that the mind, which he never sees break up, is permanent. A sub-commentary explains his mistake. Each mind
breaks up only as the proximity condition of the next, so the next one's arising covers the last one's absence; what
shows is presence, and never absence. *"This is made plain by the sight of the firebrand wheel."*[^2] A burning stick
whirled in the dark looks like a ring of fire. A stream of minds, each gone as soon as it comes, looks like one lasting
mind.

**The river.** The life-continuum, already met, flows *"like a river's stream."*[^7]

Swap the silicon back for a lamp, a burning stick or a river, and every lesson so far survives: separate moments,
joined without a seam, neither the same nor another, and seeming to be one lasting thing. None of it needs a computer.
The seeming is the start of the next chapter's question, and Chapter 4 follows how it is built.

## What the machine adds

One thing does.

The old pictures cannot pause. Put the lamp out and light it again, and you have a new flame that you lit. Stop
whirling the stick, and the wheel is gone. None of them can show an order that carries straight on across a stretch in
which the thing itself is not running, while time runs on somewhere else.

A computer can. Put it to sleep and wake it. Its next instruction follows its last, with none between them, in the
order its program runs. By the machine's own reckoning nothing happened. By the wall clock, an hour or a week went by.

```
                     ACROSS CESSATION                 A COMPUTER ASLEEP

in order             the next mind, with nothing      the next instruction,
                     of the stream between            with none between

by the wall clock    days pass                        an hour or a week passes
                     (Buddhaghosa)

by what runs         no interval of time: no          no time at all:
                     state of mind occurred           no step ran
                     (the Mūlaṭīkā)
```

That is the split between order and time, and it falls exactly where the commentators parted. Read the picture with
Buddhaghosa, and the wall clock is right: time passed, and proximity was never a matter of time. Read it with the
Mūlaṭīkā, and the machine's own reckoning is right: time is told only by what runs, and nothing ran. The picture holds
both readings at once and does not say which clock is time. That is all a picture should do.

## Others who have looked

The philosopher Henri Bergson, in *Creative Evolution* (1907), compared the intellect to a cinematograph. It takes
snapshots of a reality that flows and strings them together, so that real becoming slips away between the frames.[^17]
The Abhidhamma runs the other way. For Bergson the flow is real and the frames are the mind's artifact. For the texts
the moments are real, and the flow is what is designated on them. Each view is the other turned over, and it helps to
see them side by side.

Alexander von Rospatt's *The Buddhist Doctrine of Momentariness* (1995) traces how the doctrine of moments took shape
in the early Buddhist schools.[^18] Paul Griffiths' *On Being Mindless* (1986) studies the attainment of cessation, and
the question of how mind returns after it, across three Buddhist schools.[^19] An earlier study in this same library
follows the bridge across cessation and death in a debate about time.[^20]

## Where the simile breaks

**A frame is a still; a moment is an event.** A frame of film is one picture, the same from edge to edge for as long as
it lasts. A moment of mind, in the texts, has three phases of its own: arising, standing and dissolving.[^21] It is
something happening, never a picture held.

**Frames sit side by side; the texts leave the join open.** A sub-commentary says that the later moment arises *"at
the same time as the earlier one's dissolution, or right after it."*[^22] It gives both and chooses neither. Frames, or
the steps of a program, would settle what the texts leave open.

**A clock counts a time that runs without it.** The computer's wall clock keeps going while the machine sleeps. In the
Mūlaṭīkā's reading there is no such clock: time is said only of states as they occur.[^15]

**Matter has a gaplessness of another kind.** The manual says the matter of a body flows on through a life *"like a
lamp's flame, like a river."*[^23] That is a different continuity. Matter has no proximity condition, and its moments
overlap, in *"a connection like a chain's."*[^24] The texts use the lamp and the river for both, and the two
continuities are not the same. This chapter's rule is a rule for mind.
