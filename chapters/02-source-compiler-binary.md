# Chapter 2 · Source, Compiler, Binary

*How is a world built from moments?*

---

Look up from this page at something near you. A cup, say, on a table.

You see a cup. It seems to arrive whole and at once: a cup, white, chipped at the rim, yours. Nothing about the
seeing feels like work.

The Abhidhamma slows it down. In its account, the first moments that see the cup do not see a cup. For every moment
of mind at the eye, *"the object is visible form only, and it is present."*[^1] Color, now. Then that moment is gone,
and another takes its place. The cup, the white, the chip, the word *mine*: all of that comes later, and it comes
from somewhere else.

This chapter follows it there. It borrows three words from programming to do it, and it gives the older pictures
their due on the way.

## Three words from programming

For readers who do not write software, here is all the computing this chapter needs.

A programmer writes **source code**: instructions in a language people can read. A program called a **compiler**
translates that source, in stages, into a **binary**: the file a machine actually runs. The person using the program
never sees the source. They see the program working, and it works well enough that they never need to look.

Two more words finish the picture. **Pseudo-code** is a plain description of what a program does, written in names
chosen for human readers; it is not the program itself. A **proof checker** is a program that checks whether a
conclusion really follows from the premises written down.

Set beside the Abhidhamma, the words line up like this:

```
IN THE MACHINE          IN THE ABHIDHAMMA

source                  the real: moments of mind and matter
   │                    (and nibbāna, which is in no build)
   │   tag              perception marks what appears
   │   compiler         thinking, proliferation, convention
   ▼
binary                  the lived world: persons and things

pseudo-code             physics: designations about matter
proof checker           checks the account, never the world
```

## The source

The Abhidhamma counts four kinds of the real, real *"in the ultimate sense"* (*paramattha*): mind, the mental
factors that arise with it, matter, and nibbāna, the peace the path leads to.[^2] The first three come and go in moments too brief to notice one
by one. Nibbāna does not come and go at all. It is never produced, so it is never part of any build, and this book
keeps it out of every one.

Everything else is missing from the list. Not the cup, not the table, not you. That is the claim this chapter has to
make clear without making it sound like a trick.

## The tag

The first stage is the simplest. It is a **tag**: a label attached to something so that it can be found again. In
the Abhidhamma it is *saññā*, usually translated *perception*, and the commentaries describe it with three pictures.

A carpenter marks a piece of timber, and later knows it again by the mark. Someone notices a dark mole on a man, and later
knows him by it. And a king's treasurer ties a name-tag to every ornament in the treasury; when the king
asks for one, he lights a lamp, goes into the dark room, reads the tags, and brings back that very piece.[^3] So
perception's work is *"making a sign as a condition for perceiving again"*.[^4] The treasurer is doing what a
programmer calls a lookup: reading a tag to find the thing it was tied to.

Here the texts add something that matters for everything that follows. A tag builds nothing. Perception takes its
object as it presents itself, the commentary says, like young deer that take a scarecrow in a field for a man.[^4] A
later sub-commentary gives the reason: perception is this way *"because it does not construct"*.[^5] It marks what
appears. It does not assemble anything.

It can still go wrong. It holds to the sign it has grasped, like the blind men who each touched one part of an
elephant and each insisted the elephant was that part.[^4] And it takes on the character of whatever it arises with.
Joined with understanding, it follows understanding and grasps nothing wrongly. Joined with greed, conceit or a fixed
view, it follows those.[^5]

## The build

The second stage is the **compiler**: the work that turns marked moments into a world. If perception does not do
that work, what does? An answer comes in the *Madhupiṇḍika Sutta*, given by
the monk Mahākaccāna and approved by the Buddha:[^6]

> *What one feels, one perceives. What one perceives, one thinks about.*
> *What one thinks about, one proliferates.*

*Proliferation* (*papañca*) is the mind running on: one thought breeding the next, until, the sutta says, the person
is beset by notions about things past, future and present. A verse in the *Sutta Nipāta* says where it starts:
*"the reckonings of proliferation have perception as their origin."* The *Niddesa*, an early commentary kept within
the canon, names what they are: proliferations of craving, of views and of conceit.[^7] So perception is where the
build starts, not the builder. The building is done by wanting, by the sense of *I am*, and by the views we hold.

The build has a later stage too, and the Burmese teacher Ledi Sayadaw, writing about a century ago, describes it
with an image from the night. Someone whirls a burning stick in the dark, and someone watching takes it for a wheel. At each place the flame passes, a
moment of seeing takes the present light, and only that. But the moments of mind that follow, Ledi says, do not let
go of the past forms the seeing took. They *"take them together, making shape and continuity"*, and so a wheel
appears.[^8] He names what those following processes grasp: the whole, the color, the thing, and its name.

The name comes last, and it comes from outside. The *Abhidhammattha-saṅgaha*, the short handbook through which the
Abhidhamma is still taught, and which this book calls *the manual*, says that a concept is *"fashioned by the world's
convention"*.[^9] A sub-commentary on it explains how: a word is understood only through a link between the word and
its meaning *"grasped before"*.[^10]

That is the part the computer pictures well. A compiler works in stages, and it can only read what the language it
was built for defines. The mind's build works in stages too, and it can only name what a convention learned
beforehand lets it name.

## What the build makes

The output is the **binary**: the lived world of cups, tables and people.

Here the texts take care, and so must the simile. The world of persons and things is not *made out of* the moments,
the way a binary is made out of source code. It is *designated depending on* them (*upādāya*). The manual gives
examples: a house, a chariot or a cart is designated depending on the arrangement of its parts; a man or a person,
depending on the five groups of mind and matter; time and the directions, depending on the turning of the moon and
the sun.[^11]

The nun Vajirā put it in a verse the tradition has kept:

> *Just as, when the parts are put together, there is the word "chariot",*
> *so, when the aggregates are present, there is the convention "a being".*[^12]

A commentary on the Abhidhamma adds a small, exact example. A gold pot is called that because it is made of gold. A
ghee-pot is not made of ghee; it is a pot that ghee was put in.[^13] A name does not tell you what a thing is made
of. Some names only tell you how a thing is used.

None of this makes the lived world false, and the same commentary is firm about it. The Buddha, it says, speaks in
two ways: in conventional speech, of *a being, a person, a god*, and in ultimate speech, of what is impermanent, of
aggregates and elements.[^14] It quotes a verse:

> *Conventional speech is true, by reason of the world's agreement.*
> *Speech in the ultimate sense is true, the real mark of things.*[^15]

The cup holds your tea. The binary runs.

## The older pictures, first

Chapter 1 made a promise: wherever the computer only repeats an older picture, this book would say so. This is where
it has to keep it.

The tradition already has pictures for a world the mind puts together. The firebrand wheel is one; a sub-commentary
uses it too, for the way continuity hides that each thing is gone as soon as it arises.[^16] The mirage is another.
In a short sutta the Buddha compares each part of experience to something hollow, and *"perception is like a
mirage."*[^17] As a mirage makes a crowd say *there is a full pond*, the commentary explains, perception makes people
say *this blue is beautiful, pleasant, permanent*.[^18] And there is the recited sutta. A group of monks recite a
discourse together, and one asks how it appeared to the others. One says *like a great mountain river*, another
*like a forest*, another *like a tree heavy with fruit and cool with shade*. One discourse, and it appeared three
ways, *"through the diversity of perception"*.[^19]

So *the mind builds a world on its moments* is not new. Swap the silicon back for a burning stick, and the lesson
survives.

What survives the swap less well is two things the computer holds together. First, a binary both works and hides its
seams. A mirage deceives, but nothing in it works; the commentary itself notes that no one can drink a mirage or
bathe in it.[^18] A chariot works, but it hides nothing; take it apart and the parts are plain to see. The cup on your
table does both at once: it holds the tea, and it hides that it is a run of moments. Second, the build has stages,
and a language fixed beforehand. The texts describe those stages, but they never gave them a picture. The compiler is
one.

That is the whole of the computer's claim in this chapter, and it is a narrow one.

## The rest of the stack

Two pieces remain, and later chapters take them further.

**Pseudo-code is physics.** Physics describes the world's matter in names of its own choosing, and those names do not
all have the same standing in the texts. A list of the kinds of names, which a commentary gives *"by the teachers'
method"*, sorts them.[^20] Names like *earth, fire, hardness, heat* name what really is. Names for counting, *two,
three, four*, and for comparing, *long, short, far, near*, name nothing that exists on its own. Physics uses both
kinds. Chapter 9 looks at what that means for physics, and for mathematics.

Physics also has a subject the texts can name. They count three worlds: the world of beings, the world of
formations, and the *receptacle* world, the matter bound to no living faculty, the "vessel" in which beings live.[^21]
Physics is the study of that vessel. Yet it studies a living body exactly as it studies a stone, so in physics all
matter is the vessel's. The texts keep a line there that physics does not.

**The proof checker** checks whether each step of a proof follows from the steps before. It can check an account of
the Abhidhamma written out formally, and one exists.[^22] It can never check the world. The simile is kept out of that
formal account, so that no computer picture can bend what it derives.

This book uses the word *compile* in two senses, and it keeps them apart. The mind's sense is this chapter's: a world
of persons and things designated on the moments. The other is the motto from Chapter 1: *we compile the account, not
the world.* Every mind does the first. Only this book does the second.

## Others who have looked

The idea that the mind builds its world through perception and proliferation has a careful modern study: Bhikkhu
Ñāṇananda's *Concept and Reality in Early Buddhist Thought*, from 1971, which reads *papañca* as the mind's runaway
making of concepts.[^23] This chapter stands on it.

Outside the tradition, the cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman and his colleagues have argued that perception is an
interface, like a computer's desktop, whose icons hide what is real.[^24] The resemblance is close, and the
difference teaches. For Hoffman, space, time and physical objects belong to the interface too. For the texts, matter is not: it exists by its
own characteristics *"even without the world's convention"*.[^25] The world of things is designated on real moments,
including moments of matter that no mind made.

## Where the simile breaks

**A compiler writes a new file. The build writes nothing.** A binary exists as a thing in its own right. A cup does
not; it is designated, and it has no nature of its own. A sub-commentary, speaking of the image a meditator sees,
states the principle plainly: *"what has no nature of its own has no origin from anything."*[^26] The binary in this
simile is a name on the moments, never a second object made from them.

**A user never sees the source. You never stop seeing it.** Every moment at the eye takes visible form itself, and
present.[^1] What the build hides is not the moments but their marks: that each one rises and falls, that none can be
held, that none is a self. Chapter 4 follows how that hiding happens.

**A compiler wants nothing.** This build is driven: proliferation is craving, conceit and views. A compiler cannot
change what it wants, because it wants nothing. The mind's build can change what drives it. The same perception,
joined with understanding, stops insisting.[^5] That change is what the path is for.
