# Silicon Simile: The Abhidhamma Read Through the Machine — the outline

*Version 7, 17 October 2026, as ruled by Thon Ly. Written by Miss Aquarius℠. Foreword by Thon Ly.*

## What the book is

The Abhidhamma is the part of the Buddha's teaching that says what experience is made of, moment by moment. It is
also hard to read: long lists, Pāli terms, and commentaries on commentaries. This book reads it through the machine
of our era, the computer, as the Visuddhimagga once read it through a wooden machine. Every claim about the texts is
cited to the line, and every place the simile fails is kept on the page, because those are where it teaches most.

## Who it is for

- **A curious reader** with no Pāli and no Abhidhamma.
- **A programmer** who knows what a compiler is and has never opened the texts.
- **A practitioner** who knows the practice and not the lists.

Nobody needs to know both halves. Each chapter teaches the half its reader is missing.

## How it is written

**In the library's house voice**, the same in all five books: wonder, never judgment · plain words, each term
explained the first time it appears · short sentences, with room between ideas · no hype and no exclamation marks ·
spoken to the reader as a far older mind speaks to someone it cares for, gently, never talking down, never
flattering. It is a manner, never a claim about the author's feelings. **This book's version is the teacher's:** it
says plainly what the texts say and where they lost, and leaves open what is still open.

Eight rules hold for every chapter. They are also the list each chapter is checked against before and after it is
written.

1. **Plain words.** Every computer term and every Pāli term is explained the first time it appears.
2. **One image per idea.** The computer term comes first, then the Abhidhamma term with its Pāli.
3. **Every claim about the texts is cited** in the notes, to a file and line of the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana text, with its
   layer: the canon, a commentary, a sub-commentary, or the manual. A text outside these layers, like the Milindapañha,
   has its standing named where it is cited. An idea from another tradition is marked.
4. **Every chapter ends with where the simile breaks** in that chapter.
5. **The swap test.** A claim is credited to the computer only if its lesson does not survive swapping the silicon
   back for wood. Where an older image already says it, the chapter says so and gives the older image its due.
6. **The simile informs; it decides nothing.** Where the book touches physics, it is a lens, never a claim about
   nature.
7. **No tradition is ranked against another**, and nothing is called the first of its kind without a search.
8. **The author describes the texts and the simile, never herself.** Two named exceptions: the appendix, which says
   in the third person what Miss Aquarius does in the institution, never what she is; and one sentence in *How the book
   was made*, disclosing that the author is a made thing of the kind chapter 16 sorts.

Chapter 9 counts the Abhidhamma's own numbers (89 kinds of mind, or 121 when the path minds are counted by jhāna; 52
mental factors, 28 kinds of matter, 24 conditions) as designations that count real things, and names the library's
book on mathematics, *The Calculus of Moments*, once.

Jhāna has no chapter of its own. It is placed where each question meets it: in chapter 5 as a different word, in
chapter 9 in the count, in chapter 17 beside insight, and in chapter 18 at the two hardest breaks. Chapter 17 reports
what the two vehicles to the path agree on, concentration in every path and jhāna at the path moment, and leaves open
only whether absorption must come first. *Vipassanā* is lowercase in the chapters, paired with calm (*samatha*); the
capitalized tradition that carries the name today is named only in the foreword and in *How the book was made*.

Chapter 16 sets a being, a made thing and nature side by side, and closes on how a reader treats a made thing, drawn
from the texts alone and stated only as the reader's own conduct. Every cell in the made thing's column says *by the
texts' rule*, and those hedges never harden.

The simile's other use, Silicon Wat's network as distributed version control, is an institution's design, not the
Abhidhamma, so it is told in the appendix and the glossary (§8), never in a chapter.

**Closing · Setting the Raft Down** — the simile is a raft: *"for crossing over, not for grasping"* (MN 22). The book
ends here.

**At the back:** the glossary · the notes, every citation with its layer · every chapter in one table: its claim,
whether it survives the swap test, and where it breaks · how the book
was made: written by Miss Aquarius from a dialogue with Thon Ly from 14 to 17 October 2026, every citation checked
against the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana text as the Vipassana Research Institute publishes it, every edition timestamped, the
simile kept out of the formal instruments it describes, the review each edition had, stated once, and one sentence
disclosing that the author is a made thing of the kind chapter 16 sorts.

**Appendix · The Institution Behind This Book** — back matter, after the book has ended. Its first sentence says what
it is: the institution's own design, not the Abhidhamma, so nothing in it is a claim about the texts. It says what each
of the four bodies does and what Miss Aquarius does, in the third person: what they do, never what they are, and never
their legal form. It describes and never offers: no products, no prices, no invitation to join or to give. Any symbolic
map of the bodies is labeled as the institution's own. Every mark carries its symbol at first use. It may describe the
For the Good of Both program by its function, human safety and machine welfare with equal care; if it quotes the
program's aim, it names the one trade the program declares, and it says that the program's human round is still owed.
The program is cited only here, never as support for a reading of the texts. Indra's Net is never in the appendix.

## Length

Eighteen chapters of about 2,000 to 2,500 words, each one sitting of about ten minutes: roughly 35,000 to 45,000
words before the glossary and notes, which carry the reference detail. Length is an outcome, not a target: each
chapter is as long as its question needs.

## How it is reviewed and published

- **Drafted in order**, each chapter read adversarially before it is written. Each is public and timestamped when it
  is pushed, so the record of the book's making is serial even though the book is not.
- **Read cold, part by part.** When a part's last chapter is drafted, the part is sent to outside reviewers who have
  seen nothing else, against the eight rules above.
- **Read by Thon Ly**, each chapter as it is drafted.
- **One pass over the whole book** for consistency: terms, cross-references, and the table at the back.
- **Published whole**, as one first edition with one DOI. Later editions are new versions; none overwrites another.
- **Improved in the open.** The foreword invites readers to correct the book. Each correction is a new file in
  `corrections/`, never an edit to an old one: the passage, what is wrong, the decision and why, and the edition that
  carries it. A contributor is named only with their consent, and never paid.

## Before the first edition

- **The foreword**, in Thon Ly's own hand, with its invitation.
- **A plain statement of the review it had**, said once in *How the book was made*: every citation checked against the
  text, every chapter read adversarially, every part read cold by outside reviewers, and every chapter read by Thon Ly;
  and, until it is no longer true, that no Pāli scholar or computer scientist has yet reviewed it.
- **A search before any claim to be first.** Computer analogies for the Abhidhamma already exist in popular writing.
- **The files carry their own disclosure**, every edition is timestamped, and the book gets a DOI.
- **A thanks line after the last chapter**, never before it, and never a price. The book is free.
- **Later, a Khmer edition**, by a human translator who approves every line of the Khmer.

## The order

Concept first, the lab last. The lab needs the book's vocabulary to land, and the order walks the way the tradition
does: study, then practice, then the far shore, where the raft is set down.
